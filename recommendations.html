Part 4 · Recommendations

So what should
we do about it?

We don't think facial recognition needs to disappear completely. Face ID shows it can be done in a way that respects people. But Rite Aid and the Detroit arrests show what happens when there are no real rules. Here's what we think should change, at three different levels.

🛠️

Technology

Build the tech itself to be safer and fairer

 🤝

Social & company practices

Change how people and businesses actually use it

 🏛️

Policy & law

Set clear rules that everyone has to follow
Level 1 · Technology

Build it better from the start

Some of the problems we found can be reduced by designing the technology more carefully.

1

Keep face data on the device when possible

Face ID proves this works. If your faceprint never leaves your phone, it can't end up in a giant database or get leaked in a hack.

Fixes: data you can't reset, privacy
2

Test for bias before and after launch

Companies should test their systems on different races, genders, and ages, and keep testing after rollout. Rite Aid didn't properly test its system at all.

Fixes: bias, unequal harm
3

Refuse blurry or low-quality images

If a photo is too blurry, the system should say "can't tell" instead of guessing. Bad photos were a huge reason Rite Aid had so many false alerts.

Fixes: false matches
4

Show how confident the match is

Instead of just saying "MATCH," systems should show a confidence score and clearly warn that it's a possible lead, not proof.

Fixes: people trusting the machine too much
Level 2 · Social & company practices

Put humans back in charge

Even good technology can cause harm if the people using it don't use it responsibly.

5

Tell people when they're being scanned

Clear signs at the entrance at the very least. Better yet, ask for consent. People deserve to know before their face gets compared to a list.

Fixes: no consent, no warning
6

A human must double-check every match

No one should get searched, kicked out, or arrested just because a computer said so. A trained person should review the match and look for other evidence first.

Fixes: false accusations, wrongful arrests
7

Train the employees who use it

Workers need to understand that these systems make mistakes, especially with certain groups, and know what to do (and not do) when they get an alert.

Fixes: overtrusting the system
8

Give people a way to fight back

If you're wrongly flagged, you should be able to find out why, challenge it, and get your face removed from the database.

Fixes: no way to appeal
Level 3 · Policy & law

Make the rules official

Company promises only go so far. Rite Aid only stopped because the government stepped in. We think clear laws are the most important piece, because they apply to everyone.

9

A national biometric privacy law

The U.S. doesn't have one federal law protecting face data. We think there should be one that requires companies to get people's permission before collecting their faceprints.

Fixes: consent everywhere, not just some states
10

No arrests based only on a face match

Detroit already agreed to this after Robert Williams' case. We think it should be the rule for every police department in the country.

Fixes: wrongful arrests
11

Limit live scanning in public spaces

Scanning crowds in real time, like on streets or at protests, should be heavily restricted, and allowed only in serious, specific situations with a judge's approval.

Fixes: mass surveillance, chilling free speech
12

Independent audits and public reports

Organizations using facial recognition should be audited by outside experts and publish how often their systems are wrong, and for whom.

Fixes: hidden bias, no accountability

These ideas already exist somewhere

We didn't make these up out of nowhere. Illinois already has the Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA), which requires companies to get written consent before collecting things like faceprints. The European Union's AI Act bans some uses of facial recognition, like scraping face images from the internet to build databases, and strictly limits real-time face scanning in public. Our recommendations are basically about taking ideas like these and making them the standard across the U.S.

Who's responsible?

Everyone has a part to play

🏢

Companies

  • Test for bias before launching
  • Be honest with customers
  • Keep data on devices when possible
🏛️

Government

  • Pass a national biometric privacy law
  • Set rules for police use
  • Enforce them, like the FTC did with Rite Aid
🙋

Us (regular people)

  • Check app and phone privacy settings
  • Ask stores if they use face scanning
  • Support local laws about it
Wrapping up

Final thoughts

When we started this project, most of us just thought of facial recognition as "that thing that unlocks my phone." Now we see it a lot differently. The same technology can be convenient and respectful, or it can secretly scan people, make mistakes, and hurt the people who are already treated unfairly.

Through the utilitarian lens, we saw that careless uses like Rite Aid's cause more harm than good. Through the rights-based lens, we saw that even an accurate system is wrong if people never got a choice. Our recommendations try to respect both: protect people's rights first, then make sure the tech actually helps.

Your face belongs to you. Any technology that uses it should have to earn your trust first.

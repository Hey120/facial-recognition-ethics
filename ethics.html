Part 3 · Critical discussion

Is facial recognition
right or wrong?

Honestly, it depends on how you look at it. In this part we first lay out the bigger social and political concerns we found, and then we judge our case studies using two different ethical lenses: utilitarianism and rights-based ethics. Spoiler: they don't always agree.

The bigger picture

What this tech means for society

When we started researching, we thought the main problem was just "privacy." But the more we read, the more we realized facial recognition touches a lot of different issues, some social, some political, and some that are built right into how the technology works.

👁️ Social

The end of being anonymous

Usually when you walk around in public, strangers don't know who you are. Facial recognition can change that, linking your face to your name, where you've been, and when.

📢 Political

Chilling free speech

If people know they could be identified at a protest or rally, some might just stay home. Even if no one gets punished, the fear alone can stop people from speaking up.

⚖️ Built-in risk

Bias in the algorithm

NIST found many algorithms had 10 to 100 times more false positives for Asian and African American faces than white faces. So the harm isn't spread out evenly.

🚓 Political

Police power

When police use it, a wrong match isn't just embarrassing. It can mean getting arrested for something you didn't do (see the story below).

✋ Social

No real way to opt out

You can skip a website or delete an app. But you can't really avoid having a face. Rite Aid shoppers didn't even know they were being scanned.

🔓 Built-in risk

Data you can't reset

If a database of faceprints gets hacked or misused, there's no "change your password" option. Your face is permanent.

Real story: arrested in front of his family

In January 2020, Detroit police arrested Robert Williams, a Black man, at his home in front of his wife and young daughters. Facial recognition had wrongly matched his driver's license photo to a man in security video from a watch store. The ACLU says his was the first publicly reported case in the U.S. of someone being wrongfully arrested because of facial recognition.

In 2024, Detroit settled his lawsuit for $300,000 and agreed that police can no longer arrest people based only on facial recognition results. Williams is one of at least three people wrongfully arrested this way in Detroit, and all three were Black.

Our two ethical lenses

Two ways of asking "is this okay?"

An ethical lens is basically a way of thinking through a moral question. Different lenses focus on different things, so the same situation can look fine through one lens and totally wrong through another.

Lens 1

Utilitarianism

"Does it create more good than harm overall?"

  • Focuses on outcomes and consequences
  • Adds up the benefits and harms for everyone affected
  • The right choice is whatever produces the most overall good
  • A few people being harmed can be acceptable if many more people benefit

Linked to philosophers Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill.

Lens 2

Rights-based ethics

"Does it respect every person's rights?"

  • Focuses on people's rights and dignity, not just results
  • Everyone has rights like privacy, consent, and fair treatment
  • Those rights can't be traded away just because it helps the majority
  • People should be treated as individuals, not just as data points

Linked to ideas from Immanuel Kant and modern human rights thinking.

Utilitarianism up close

Weighing Rite Aid on the scale

A utilitarian would try to put everything on a scale. On one side: less shoplifting, safer employees, lower costs. On the other: thousands of false alerts, innocent people searched and kicked out, and an 11-year-old treated like a criminal.

You could imagine someone at Rite Aid arguing the benefits were worth it. But once you add up all the harms, including the ones that don't show up on a sales report, the scale tips the other way. Click to see.

What we think

Our team's take

After going back and forth (and honestly, we didn't all agree at first), we landed on this: utilitarianism is useful for showing that Rite Aid's system did more harm than good. But we think it misses something. Under utilitarianism, Rite Aid's system could have been "okay" if it had just been a bit more accurate, even if it still scanned everyone without asking.

The rights-based lens catches what utilitarianism misses. The problem with Rite Aid wasn't only that it made mistakes. It's that people never got a say in the first place. And the people who got hurt the most were groups that already deal with unfair treatment.

The real difference between Face ID and Rite Aid isn't the technology. It's consent and control.

That's why we think the best approach uses both lenses together. Rights should set the baseline (consent, fairness, the right to challenge a match), and then within those limits, we can ask whether a specific use actually does more good than harm. That idea shapes all of our recommendations on the next page.

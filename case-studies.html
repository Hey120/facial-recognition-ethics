Part 2 · Real-world examples

Same technology,
two very different stories

To really understand facial recognition, we looked at two real companies that use it. One is something most of us carry in our pocket. The other ended with a federal ban. The tech underneath is pretty similar. What's different is who is in control and whether people even knew.

Case study 1

Apple Face ID: your face as your key

Apple introduced Face ID in 2017 with the iPhone X, and now it's how millions of people unlock their phones, pay with Apple Pay, and log into apps. A lot of us honestly don't remember the last time we typed our passcode.

What makes Face ID interesting for our project is that Apple designed it with privacy in mind. Your face data doesn't get sent to Apple. It stays locked inside a special chip on your phone.

🔦

Projects dots

The TrueDepth camera shines over 30,000 invisible infrared dots onto your face to build a 3D map.

🔐

Locks it away

The math version of your face is encrypted and kept in the phone's Secure Enclave, not on Apple's servers or in iCloud.

👀

Checks attention

By default it only unlocks if your eyes are open and looking at the phone, so it's harder to unlock while you're asleep.

Why this case matters

Face ID is probably the best-case version of facial recognition. It's opt-in, the data stays with you, and if it messes up, the worst thing that happens is you type your passcode. We included it because you can't really judge the bad uses of this tech without seeing what a more careful version looks like.

But it's not perfect. Here are the challenges:

Twins and kids

Apple itself says the chance of a false match is higher for twins, siblings who look alike, and children under 13 whose faces are still developing.

Can you be forced to unlock?

A passcode lives in your head, but your face is just... there. U.S. courts haven't fully agreed on whether police can make someone unlock a phone with their face or fingerprint.

Making face scanning feel normal

When unlocking your phone with your face becomes routine, people might get comfortable with face scanning everywhere, even in places where it's way less safe.

It's still your body as a password

If biometric data ever leaks, you can't reset it. Apple's design limits this risk, but other companies copying the idea might not be as careful.

1 in 1,000,000

Apple's estimate of the chance that a random person could unlock your phone with their face. For comparison, Apple says it's about 1 in 50,000 for Touch ID fingerprints.

0

The number of times your Face ID data gets uploaded to Apple. It never leaves your device.

2017

The year Face ID launched with the iPhone X. It's now on most iPhones and iPad Pros.

Case study 2

Rite Aid: when the store is watching you

Rite Aid is a big U.S. pharmacy chain. For about eight years, it secretly used facial recognition in hundreds of its stores to try to catch shoplifters. The goal sounds reasonable, right? Stores lose a lot of money to theft. But the way Rite Aid did it ended up hurting a lot of innocent people.

Rite Aid hired two companies to build a database of "persons of interest," people it believed had committed or tried to commit crimes in its stores. The database had images of tens of thousands of people, pulled from security cameras, employees' phone cameras, and even news stories. A lot of those photos were blurry or low quality.

How a false alert turned into a real problem

Blurry photo added to the database
→
Innocent shopper walks in
→
System says "match!"
→
Employee acts on the alert
→
Shopper followed, searched, kicked out, or reported to police

Timeline

2012

Rite Aid starts using facial recognition in its stores. Customers aren't told.

2012–2020

The system creates thousands of false matches. Employees follow, search, and accuse customers, sometimes in front of their friends and family.

2020

Rite Aid stops using the technology.

Dec 2023

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) bans Rite Aid from using facial recognition for 5 years and orders it to delete the images it collected.

1,000+

Match alerts the system generated for the same one person in about two months, after a single photo was uploaded in the Bronx. That was nearly 5% of all of Rite Aid's alerts in that period.

Tens of thousands

The number of people whose images ended up in Rite Aid's database.

5 years

The length of the FTC's ban on Rite Aid using facial recognition.

The story that stuck with us

According to the FTC, a Rite Aid employee stopped and searched an 11-year-old girl because the system wrongly matched her face to someone in the database. Her mom said she had to miss work because her daughter was so upset about what happened.

For us, this one example says a lot. A computer made a guess, a person trusted it without questioning it, and a kid paid the price.

The social challenges in this case

No consent, no warning

Customers had no idea their faces were being scanned and compared to a list. You can't object to something you don't know about.

The harm wasn't equal

The FTC found the false matches hit people of color and women harder. This connects to research we mentioned on the homepage showing many systems are less accurate on these groups.

Bad photos in, bad results out

Using blurry, low-quality images made false matches way more likely. The FTC also said Rite Aid didn't properly test how accurate the system was, before or after rolling it out.

People trusted the machine too much

Employees were acting on alerts without really double-checking them. When a computer says "match," it feels official, even when it's wrong.

Putting it together

So why is this tech so hard to use well?

Looking at both cases, we noticed it's not really the algorithm alone that decides whether facial recognition is helpful or harmful. It's the whole setup around it. Face ID works pretty well because it's comparing you to one clear, 3D scan of yourself, you agreed to it, and a mistake costs you about two seconds.

Rite Aid flipped all of that. Blurry photos, a huge database, no consent, and real consequences for being wrong. The bigger the database and the worse the photos, the more likely it is that the system finds someone who just kind of looks like you. And when the people getting flagged are already more likely to be misidentified, the tech ends up making existing unfairness worse.

That raises the big question for the next part of our project: is facial recognition okay as long as the benefits outweigh the harms? Or do people have rights over their own faces no matter what?
